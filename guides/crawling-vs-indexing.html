ReIndexMe · Guide 01

Crawling is not indexing

A crawler can fetch your page without Google adding it to its index. And an indexed page may still not appear for the search you try.

Read guide ↓← Back to guides
Key takeaway

A successful crawl is one step, not a promise of search visibility.

Four different events

When a page changes, several different things must happen. These are related, but they are not the same event.

01 · DiscoveryThe URL is found

Through links, earlier crawls, sitemaps or other signals.

02 · CrawlingThe page is fetched

A crawler requests the URL and may render its content.

03 · IndexingContent is evaluated

The search engine processes the page and decides whether to store it, potentially choosing another canonical URL.

04 · ServingA result may appear

The indexed content may be shown for relevant searches, depending on query and other factors.

Google explicitly says that not every page progresses through every stage. A page can be known to Google without being indexed.

Why a crawled page may not be indexed

For example, a crawler may read a page but decide not to keep it as an independent search result.

  • noindex: a robots meta directive or HTTP X-Robots-Tag can instruct a compliant search engine not to index it.
  • Duplicate or similar content: the search engine may select another URL as the canonical version.
  • Insufficient value or processing issues: crawling a document does not guarantee inclusion in the index.
  • Technical responses: server errors, access restrictions or missing resources can prevent reliable processing.
Important distinction

robots.txt controls crawling, not indexing directly. If a page is blocked from crawling, Google may be unable to read a noindex directive on it. A blocked URL can sometimes still appear in search results without its content.

Indexed does not mean visible for every query

Even an indexed page is not guaranteed to appear when you search for a particular phrase. Google selects results based on relevance and many other signals. A title or snippet shown in search may also differ from what you wrote in the page metadata.

Separate these questions: “Can Google index this URL?”, “Did Google index it?”, and “Does it appear for this specific search?” Each needs a different check.

How to check a page you manage

  1. Open the live URL. Confirm the correct content was published and the page loads normally.
  2. Inspect on-page signals. Check the canonical URL, title, robots meta tag and internal links. ReIndexMe Local Inspector can help with browser-visible signals.
  3. Use Google Search Console. If you own or manage the site, use URL Inspection to review the indexing state and Google-selected canonical.
  4. Check server-level rules separately. Review response codes, robots.txt and HTTP headers; a browser-only inspection does not prove crawler access.
  5. Allow time and compare. Search engines make the final crawling and indexing decisions.

Local Inspector does not check the live Google index or simulate Googlebot. It is an on-page diagnostic, not an indexing certificate.

What to do next

If the page is accessible but Google has not revisited it, continue with the guide to delayed updates. If Google selected an unexpected representative URL, read the canonical URL guide. For publishing many changed pages, see sitemaps and lastmod.
